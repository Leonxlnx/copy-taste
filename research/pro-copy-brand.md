# What makes professional brand web copy good: evidence from pre-LLM sites (2014–2021)

## Method and sourcing

- Every quote below came from a Wayback Machine snapshot I fetched during this research, dated 2014–2021. I pulled each one as raw archived HTML (the `id_` form of the snapshot URL) and stripped it to visible text, headings, button labels, alt text and meta descriptions. Nothing is quoted from memory.
- The snapshot URL next to each site is the one the archive actually served. Where I asked for 2018 and got a nearby date, the served date is listed.
- Quotes are kept short (under 15 words) and mostly at headline length. Body copy is quoted in fragments and otherwise paraphrased. Straight apostrophes replace curly ones. Original capitalisation and typos ("Everything you need, with room for move.", "When bath's go badly") are kept, so you can see the copy as it was published.
- 35 sites/pages were usable. Sites that failed or were excluded are listed at the end, with reasons.

## Part 1: Site-by-site evidence

### COFFEE / FOOD

#### 1. Blue Bottle Coffee
- Home: https://web.archive.org/web/20180615224545/https://bluebottlecoffee.com/
- Our Story: https://web.archive.org/web/20180612140847/https://bluebottlecoffee.com/our-story

**Excerpts**
- Hero eyebrow + H1: "Adventure Calls for Coffee" / "Where Will Your Cold Brew Bundle Take You?"
- Tiles: "Three Africas" · "Our Brightest Blend" · "Coffee On Their Terms"
- Pull quote from a named staffer (Green Buyer Charlie Habegger): "Green coffee is a living thing, more like a produce than an inert spice"
- Story headings: "Kolshitsky & the first Blue Bottle" · "319 years later" · "May we bring you coffee?" · "Who are our people?"
- Body: "a slightly disaffected freelance musician and coffee lunatic" · "Using a minuscule six-pound batch roaster" · "I will only sell coffee less than 48 hours out of the roaster" · "Improbably and delightfully, there are hundreds of us now."
- More body: the beans left behind in Vienna were "thought to be camel feed". The early delivery model was "a fraught business model". "there is only so much coffee you can pack into your Peugeot 505 wagon"
- Support: "Reach out to Daniel, Adam, Serah, or Mandi today."
- CTAs: "Take the quiz" · "Share Coffee" · "Join us" · "Contact us" · newsletter "Sign up for stories, coffee tips, and special gear"

**Why it works**
- The origin story covers 319 years and stays concrete throughout: a date (September 13, 1683), a named man (Kolshitsky), a mistaken identity (camel feed), a machine with a weight (six-pound roaster), a car model (Peugeot 505) and a rule with a number (48 hours).
- It's humble without false modesty. The founder is a "coffee lunatic". The first business model failed and they say so. Growth is "improbable".
- The section headings are questions or odd numbers ("319 years later"). None of them is a label like "Our Values".
- Craft gets described through constraints (48 hours, six pounds). The word "artisan" never appears.
- What it doesn't say: there's no "premium", no "passion for coffee", no "world-class". Freshness is asserted once, as a vow, and then left alone.

#### 2. Stumptown Coffee Roasters
- Home: https://web.archive.org/web/20170615174406/http://www.stumptowncoffee.com/
- Our Story: https://web.archive.org/web/20170621165151/http://www.stumptowncoffee.com/our-story

**Excerpts**
- Page title: "Coffee Roasted Daily"
- Newsletter: "Get secret notes". Confirmation: "You'll start getting our secret notes in your inbox soon."
- Story opening: "Coffee is about pleasure." · "your hand is warmed by the mug, you bring it up to your nose"
- Sensory instruction: "close your eyes. Open your mouth slightly." · "You are smelling the most complex thing humans consume." · "Coffee has twice as much going on, molecularly speaking, as red wine."
- Craft: "There is one right way to roast coffee." · "Stare. Smell. Five seconds later, repeat the process" · "you should probably shave 45 seconds off that roast" · "Please don't burn those beans. They've been through so much."
- Sourcing travel: "8 vaccines". In one case, "eating the heart of a bull that has been slaughtered in your honor"
- Named buyer quote: "Basically, it's as many calories as you can cram in a bowl." (—Gabriel C., Stumptown Coffee Buyer, about Colombian sancocho)
- Values in plain words: "We don't call them farmers, we call them producers". They pay "up to four times what they could get for their crop"
- Section heading: "What does Direct Trade mean to Stumptown?"
- Producer names used as headings: "Gachatha Farmer Cooperative Society" · "Ricardo "El Tigre" Zelaya" · "Arturo Aguirre Sr. and Jr."

**Why it works**
- It's written in the second person and in the present tense. The reader is physically put through the act of tasting.
- Craft comes across as repetition and judgement: the roaster pulls samples, stares, smells, puts them back, all day. "Passion" never needs to appear.
- The numbers are human and odd: 45 seconds, 8 vaccines, four days of travel, four times the commodity price.
- It's funny at its own expense ("They've been through so much") and nobody gets sneered at.
- Direct trade is explained as a behaviour (visits, prices, equipment), not as a badge.
- What it doesn't say: there's no "ethically sourced" slogan and no certification-logo talk. It never uses "best coffee in the world" either. "The best coffee grows in the most remote places" is a fact about geography.

#### 3. Intelligentsia Coffee
- Home: https://web.archive.org/web/20160617070301/http://www.intelligentsiacoffee.com/

**Excerpts**
- Coffee names in the nav: "Tikur Anbessa Ethiopia" · "Los Delirios Nicaragua" · "Decaf Librarian's Blend" · "El Diablo Dark Roast" · "Black Cat Project Espresso"
- Cappuccino cup: "a strict 5 oz in volume" · "the comfort of the handle, the smooth bowl shaped interior base"

- T-shirt: "our love and appreciation of both black coffee and black metal"
- Cycling socks: "a five inch cuff for that perfect pro-level sock height"
- CTAs: "+ BUY" · "Learn More"

**Why it works**
- The homepage is really a catalogue, and the voice lives in the product descriptions. They're precise (5 oz, 58mm, five-inch cuff) and they name collaborators (Reg Barber, TenSpeed Hero, Golden Saddle Cyclery).
- Product names do brand work on their own ("Librarian's Blend").

#### 4. Sweetgreen
- Home: https://web.archive.org/web/20170616093621/http://www.sweetgreen.com/
- Our Story: https://web.archive.org/web/20170616203036/http://www.sweetgreen.com/our-story

**Excerpts**
- Rotating lowercase H1s, each with a link: "summer better than others" → "meet our new summer bowls→" · "our food ethos" → "what goes on the menu, and why→" · "we're looking for the cream of the crop" → "join the team→"
- App block: "Tap that app." · "we're putting healthy in your hands. Literally."
- Story: "We're in the business of feeding people" · "we're out to change what that means."
- Values as imperatives: "win, win, win" · "keep it real" · "make decisions that last longer than you will."
- Spaces: "Our guests don't go to a sweetgreen, they go to their sweetgreen" · "chalkboard source lists and open kitchens"
- Mission (weaker): "inspire healthier communities by connecting people to real food" · "impact is not an arm of our business, it is our business"

**Why it works, and where it doesn't**
- The homepage headlines are short, lowercase and pun-driven, and every one has a literal link line underneath that says exactly what you'll get ("what goes on the menu, and why"). That pairing works well: a joke headline, then a plain-English link.
- "make decisions that last longer than you will" is a values line that actually constrains behaviour.
- The mission paragraph is the weakest copy in the coffee/food set. It's abstract nouns ("impact", "transparency", "accessibility", "communities") with no person, place or number in it. Compare the chalkboard and open kitchen line, which is concrete.

#### 5. Oatly
- US home: https://web.archive.org/web/20190706085728/https://us.oatly.com/
- UK home: https://web.archive.org/web/20190110000502/https://www.oatly.com/uk/
- The Oatly Way: https://web.archive.org/web/20190722032227/https://us.oatly.com/pages/the-oatly-way

**Excerpts**
- Meta description: the company "only took 25 years or so to perfect our craft"
- The hero headline is a hand-drawn image with no HTML text. The body under it says: "We didn't write that headline because we have a special ability"
- Origin: "a country mistakenly known for polar bears roaming the streets"
- Store finder: "this incredible computerized oatmilk locator". CTA: "Computerized oatmilk locator"
- Feedback: "Chelsea, our resident feedback response unit who is actually a person" · "Or maybe just try to make her laugh, because we haven't been able to."
- Nav subheads: "More than you would ever want to know about our products." · "Potentially the most watched films about oats ever." · "The map. Whenever getting cravings for a cappoatccino."
- UK cookie banner: "Cookies go nicely with oat drinks." Button: "Ok with me, let's go!"
- UK map: "we have given it the ingenious name Oatfinder" · "Teamwork rules!"
- Oatly Way: "the guy writing this excessively long sentence" · "We are the other Swede" · "somewhat boring, super practical, painfully honest, notoriously hardworking"
- Principles: "We are not a perfect company, not even close, but our intentions are true." · "No GMOs. One more time. No GMOs." · "All we know is oats." · "Hope you are okay with that."
- Enemy: "an old man behind a wooden desk in a tall building"

**Why it works**
- The voice is self-aware about being marketing. It names the headline, the sales pitch and the long sentence. That disarms the reader's ad-scepticism.
- The superlatives are ironic ("incredible computerized oatmilk locator", "ingenious name Oatfinder"), so hype becomes a joke the reader shares in.
- One real person (Chelsea) and one specific running gag (she's never laughed).
- Rhythm alternates between one absurdly long sentence and deadpan fragments ("No GMOs. One more time. No GMOs.").
- Even the cookie banner and nav microcopy are in voice.
- What it doesn't say: it never claims to be "the best". It offers "Hope you are okay with that." Its imperfection is stated outright.

#### 6. innocent drinks
- Home: https://web.archive.org/web/20160612063913/http://www.innocentdrinks.co.uk/
- Our Story: https://web.archive.org/web/20160607083224/http://www.innocentdrinks.co.uk/us/our-story

**Excerpts**
- H1: "Hello, we're innocent". Positioning: "do themselves some good" · "(whilst making it taste nice too)"
- Newsletter H1: "Sign up for love, friendship, a weekly newsletter"
- Date-of-birth microcopy: "Sorry if this seems a bit nosy."
- Contact: "Call the bananaphone" · "Fruit Towers 342 Ladbroke Grove"
- Purpose line: "help people live well and die old"
- Origin: "Then we got people to vote with their empties."
- Timeline headings: "Stop talking, start starting…" · "The fairly boring bit" · "Thank you Mr Pinto" · "Tasty drinks, dodgy bottles" · "How to go bankrupt, quickly" · "A new dawn/juice"
- Timeline bodies: "Every bank, venture capitalist and business angel in London turns them down." · "Does anyone know anyone rich?" · "Then Hungry Aphid. Then Nude. Then Naked. Then innocent."
- Failures: "They taste great. They look nice. But they fail to set the world alight" · "We give 46% of all profits to charity and nearly bankrupt the business."
- Small numbers: "We start sending eleven people our weekly webnews." · "some of them stay subscribed for week two."
- Asides: "It's a bit like the Turin Shroud, but less old." · "PR rule number one – say sorry lots."

**Why it works**
- The About page is a timeline of anecdotes. Each one has a headline that's a joke and a body that's a fact.
- The failures are on display: a dead juice line, ugly first bottles, rejection by every investor, a near-bankruptcy, a telling-off on TV. That makes the wins (100% recycled bottles, £2.4m committed by the foundation) believable.
- People are named throughout (Jon, Rich, Adam, Mr Pinto, Caroline Nash, Ben the designer).
- Places are named down to the sandwich bar ("Out To Lunch").
- Throwaway brackets carry the humour: "(thank you New Adam)", "(Sorry.)".
- What it doesn't say: there's no "mission-driven", no "purpose" jargon, and nothing about "natural goodness". The purpose line is a joke with a point ("die old").

#### 7. Tony's Chocolonely
- Home: https://web.archive.org/web/20190716043709/https://tonyschocolonely.com/us/en/
- Mission: https://web.archive.org/web/20190629150317/https://tonyschocolonely.com/us/en/our-story

**Excerpts**
- H1: "together we'll make chocolate 100% slave free"
- Section heads: "this is what you can do!" · "share our chocolate" · "become serious friends" · "bean to bar journey"
- CTAs: "to our chocoshop!" · "support our mission and join us" · "the chocotruck is on the road" · "read our annual FAIR report" · "check out the choco numbers" · "wanna be a Tony, too?"
- Counter labels: "metric ton cocoa beans purchased" · "farmers that we work with" · "extra premium paid in dollars"
- Interactive: "which bars did you eat?" · "and how many?"
- Mission page H2: "What?! Slavery?" · "Right now there is slavery on cocoa farms in West Africa." · "It won't be easy. And it's pretty complex." · "All hands on deck!" · "We all have to roll up our sleeves.."

**Why it works**
- The mission is the headline, and it's measurable ("100%"). Everything is lowercase and has a playful tone, but the subject (slavery) is said plainly.
- Hard topic, light tone, with no euphemism. "What?! Slavery?" voices the reader's own shock.
- Proof comes as numbers (tons, farmers, premium dollars) plus an annual report CTA, not as adjectives.
- House lexicon: chocoshop, chocotruck, choco numbers, "a Tony".
- Says the hard thing honestly: "It won't be easy. And it's pretty complex."

#### 8. Dishoom (extra, not on your list)
- Home: https://web.archive.org/web/20160615163702/http://www.dishoom.com/

**Excerpts**
- Title: "Dishoom | Bombay Café". Section H3: "From Bombay with love"
- Body: "rich businessmen, sweaty taxi-wallas and courting couples" · "Fans turned slowly." · "Bentwood chairs were reflected in stained mirrors, next to sepia family portraits."
- Rhythm: "Students had breakfast. Families dined. Lawyers read briefs. Writers found their characters."
- History with numbers: almost four hundred Irani cafés at their 1960s peak. "Now, fewer than thirty remain."
- Captions: "PICTURED: The legendary B. Merwan on Bombay's Grant Road dates from 1914"
- Menu heading: "All day, every day" · "Bombay breakfast, lunch, afternoon chai, dinner & tipples"
- Press: full quotes attributed to "Richard Vines Bloomberg" and "Time Out". Awards with years: "Ranked No. 21, 2015"

**Why it works**
- A restaurant site that opens with a vanished world rather than its own food. It's an homage with specific furniture (bentwood chairs, stained mirrors, sepia portraits) and specific people (taxi-wallas, lawyers, writers).
- Four short sentences give a whole social panorama ("Students had breakfast. Families dined.").
- Captions name a real café, its street and its founding year. The photo becomes evidence.
- Press quotes are long, specific and attributed to a named critic and outlet, and awards come with years and rank.
- What it doesn't say: "authentic", "delicious", "vibrant". The authenticity is shown through history.

### CONSUMER / DTC

#### 9. Patagonia
- Home: https://web.archive.org/web/20170615191525/http://www.patagonia.com/home/
- Company Info: https://web.archive.org/web/20160616063005/http://www.patagonia.com/us/patagonia.go?assetid=2047

**Excerpts**
- Mission: "Build the best product, cause no unnecessary harm"
- Home CTAs: "Speak Up For Public Lands" · "Shop Used Gear" · "Shop Real Food" · sub-brands "Worn Wear", "The Cleanest Line" (blog), "Ironclad Guarantee"
- Reason for Being: "These are all silent sports." · "None require a motor; none deliver the cheers of a crowd." · "reward comes in the form of hard-won grace"
- Origin: "a business started by a band of climbers and surfers" · Chouinard as "an itinerant climber selling pitons from the back of his car"
- Admission: "from lighting stores to dyeing shirts – creates pollution as a by-product"
- Book title: "The Education of a Reluctant Businessman"

**Why it works**
- "Silent sports" is a coined category that explains the whole product line and the values in two words.
- The company admits its own harm, and gives specifics (lighting stores, dyeing shirts) before stating any remedy.
- The CTAs are activist and circular ("Shop Used Gear" from a company that sells new gear). That's a credibility signal no adjective could produce.
- The founder origin is one physical image: pitons, the back of a car.
- What it doesn't say: no "eco-friendly", no "green". It never calls itself "sustainable" in the hero.

#### 10. Aesop
- Home 2016: https://web.archive.org/web/20160703230459/http://www.aesop.com/fr/?___store=fr_en
- Home 2019: https://web.archive.org/web/20190616085643/https://www.aesop.com/us/
- Product page: https://web.archive.org/web/20200412142432/https://www.aesop.com/us/p/body-hand/hand/resurrection-aromatique-hand-balm/

**Excerpts**
- 2016 headings: "A first French space outside Paris" · "For skin in need" · "A sensitive restoration" · "Expressive of the craftsman's hand". CTA: "Explore the range"
- 2019 gift copy: "Gifts for those whose humorous stories brightened our days." · "In appreciation of a father's fiction"
- 2019 product headings: "For direct and incidental sunlight" · "Hydration for skin in motion" · "recirculated air drawing moisture from the surface"
- Product: "delivers rich hydration to labour-wearied hands and cuticles" · "Nourished, supple, with a greaseless finish" · "Citrus, woody, herbaceous" · "Mandarin Rind, Rosemary Leaf, Cedar Atlas" · "made from 99.7% recycled PET"
- Usage: "paying close attention to cuticles". CTA: "Add to your cart — $30.00"
- Subscription confirmation: "Sincere thanks for subscribing."
- Footer/interstitials: literary quotations (Proust in 2016, Saint-Exupéry in 2019)

**Why it works**
- A formal, literate register: "clients" not customers, "formulations" not products, "labour-wearied". It's consistent everywhere, including the thank-you message.
- Father's Day copy takes a cliché and turns it toward character ("a father's fiction", the stories he told). It's affectionate and specific without being sentimental.
- Sensory notes come in tight triads ("Citrus, woody, herbaceous"), with key ingredients named as botanicals.
- What it doesn't say: there's never an exclamation mark, a discount shout or "luxury". The price sits inside the button and nobody dresses it up.

## Part 2: Synthesis

### A. 24 actionable rules derived from the evidence

1. **Make the hero the thing itself (news, an offer, an invitation), not your category description.**
   - Glossier: "Two new ways to blush."
   - Blue Bottle: "Where Will Your Cold Brew Bundle Take You?"
   - Counter-example, Instrument: "We're a digital brand and experience innovation company".

2. **Replace evaluative adjectives with countable facts.**
   - Hoxton: "Late checkout for £5 an hour"
   - Intelligentsia: "Tamper base size is 58mm."
   - Ace: "a full 711 sq ft for all the living you need"

3. **Name real people, first names included: staff, founders, producers, customers.**
   - Blue Bottle: "Reach out to Daniel, Adam, Serah, or Mandi today."
   - Oatly: "Chelsea, our resident feedback response unit who is actually a person"

4. **Name real places down to the street, the building or the monument.**
   - Hoxton: "The original Hoxton, a firm fixture in Shoreditch since 2006."
   - Dishoom: "The legendary B. Merwan on Bombay's Grant Road dates from 1914"

5. **Put the reader's body in the scene, in second person and present tense.**
   - Stumptown: "your hand is warmed by the mug, you bring it up to your nose"
   - Barry's: "when the doors close, lights dim, and music turns up"

6. **Show craft as process, repetition and constraint, never as the word "craft".**
   - Stumptown: "Stare. Smell. Five seconds later, repeat the process"
   - Blue Bottle: "Using a minuscule six-pound batch roaster"

7. **Admit failures, flaws and limits. Admitted weakness makes the strong claims believable.**
   - innocent: "They taste great. They look nice. But they fail to set the world alight"
   - Oatly: "We are not a perfect company, not even close, but our intentions are true."
   - Patagonia names its own pollution ("from lighting stores to dyeing shirts").

8. **Define yourself by what you refuse.**
   - Hiut: "Saying no is focus."
   - Allbirds: "No flashy logos. No senseless details."

9. **Point the humour at yourself, never at the customer.**
   - Chimero: "I am still waiting for my first Kids' Choice Award."
   - Ueno: "And no, that's not how you pronounce it."

10. **Write the microcopy (cookie banners, forms, newsletter confirmations, webcams, footers) in full voice.** This is where readers notice that a human wrote the copy.
    - Oatly: "Cookies go nicely with oat drinks."
    - innocent DOB field: "Sorry if this seems a bit nosy."
    - Hiut webcam: "(The camera updates sporadically whenever it feels like it)"

11. **Write CTAs as verb + the real object. When playful, keep them literal.**
    - "Try 5 for free" (Warby Parker), "Book a room tonight" (Hoxton), "Tell us everything" (Ueno), "Computerized oatmilk locator" (Oatly), "to our chocoshop!" (Tony's).
    - Give low-intent visitors a graceful exit: Hoxton's "I'm just looking, I'm not buying".

12. **Tell the origin as a scene: a date, a place, a problem, a decision.**
    - innocent: "Then we got people to vote with their empties."
    - Warby Parker: a founder "spent the first semester of grad school without them" (his glasses)
    - Mother: "sitting around a kitchen table eating lunch"

13. **Give the story an antagonist, vivid or deliberately unnamed.**
    - Mother: "the grotesquely swollen octopus of global capital"
    - Warby Parker: "dominated by a single company"
    - Oatly: "an old man behind a wooden desk in a tall building"

14. **Use rhythm on purpose: fragment runs, triads with a surprise last item, one long sentence against a short one.**
    - Dishoom: "Students had breakfast. Families dined. Lawyers read briefs. Writers found their characters."
    - Barry's: "run faster, lift more, lean out, quiet down"
    - MailChimp: "better, weirder, and more human"

15. **Let other people say the superlatives, and attribute them fully (name, outlet or company).**
    - Dishoom credits critic "Richard Vines" at "Bloomberg"
    - Basecamp pairs "59% of our customers have fewer weekly meetings." with a named customer at a named church.
    - Ueno's only praise comes from "Jeremiah Shaw, Google".
    - Weak contrast: Freehand's unattributed "accommodates the free-spirited traveler…".

16. **Make numbers human-scaled and slightly odd. Small numbers can be funnier and more credible than big ones.**
    - innocent: "We start sending eleven people our weekly webnews."
    - Stumptown: "8 vaccines"
    - Pentagram: "Our 19 partners are all practicing designers"

17. **Name products, rooms and places with personality. Your taxonomy is copy too.**
    - Hoxton: "Shoebox, Cosy and Roomy sizes"
    - Intelligentsia: "Decaf Librarian's Blend"
    - innocent HQ: "Fruit Towers"

18. **Build a house lexicon and use it everywhere.**
    - Tony's: "chocoshop", "chocotruck", "choco numbers"
    - Oatly: "cappoatccino"
    - Mother: "Head of Mothering"

19. **Keep the mission to one concrete, falsifiable sentence.** Long mission paragraphs drift into abstract nouns.
    - Patagonia: "Build the best product, cause no unnecessary harm"
    - Tony's: "together we'll make chocolate 100% slave free"
    - Contrast Sweetgreen's mission paragraph: "inspire healthier communities by connecting people to real food".

20. **When you use a superlative, make it narrow and checkable, then prove it in the next sentence.**
    - Pentagram: "the world's largest independently-owned design studio", followed by "Our structure is unique." and the reason (the owners create the work).

21. **For luxury, use proper nouns, history and restraint. Never write "luxury" in the body.**
    - Aman: "Formerly the king's guest house" and "views of Mount Fuji". The word "luxury" appears only in its SEO title and meta.
    - Soho House: "Applicants must be nominated by two existing Soho House members."

22. **Talk to one busy reader. Acknowledge their time and their scepticism.**
    - Ueno: "We know you are busy so we'll get straight to the point."
    - Oatly: "if you think that was kind of a hard sales pitch". Basecamp: "We've been expecting you."

23. **Give the reader a small ritual or action to take part in.**
    - Stumptown: "close your eyes. Open your mouth slightly."
    - Hiut's "No Wash Club"
    - Tony's: "which bars did you eat?"

24. **Watch for drift into buzzword triads. In this set, generic copy shows up in about/mission paragraphs and press-release prose.**
    - COLLINS about: "transform brands, drive business, and improve people's lives"
    - MailChimp about: "creating innovative products that empower"
    - Equinox 2017: "Unrivaled fitness classes. Unparalleled personal training."
    - Treat those spots as the places to rewrite first.

### B. How the categories differ

| Category | Where the voice lives | Typical proof | Signature move | Typical failure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Café / coffee / food** | Origin story, sourcing pages, product names, packaging-style microcopy | Producer names, farms, regions, times and temperatures ("48 hours", "45 seconds") | Sensory walk-through of the moment of consumption (Stumptown). Historical scene-setting (Blue Bottle, Dishoom). CPG brands go full comic persona (innocent, Oatly, Tony's) | Abstract mission paragraphs (Sweetgreen). Homepage as bare catalogue (Intelligentsia) |
| **DTC / consumer** | Problem-solution origin, guarantees, product taxonomy | Numbers in the offer ("5 pairs, 5 days"), materials, % recycled, named founders with one credential | A pun or twist per heading (Allbirds), a refusal stance (Hiut, Patagonia), the customer folded into the brand (Glossier "trust us, we're you") | "Revolutionary" and "ecosystem" language. Borrowed press superlatives repeated as fact |
| **Hotel / hospitality** | Room descriptions, neighbourhood paragraph, booking microcopy | Square footage, room counts, addresses, founding year, priced perks | Rooms with personalities and objects (Ace's turntable, Hoxton's Shoebox). Place and history as luxury (Aman, Soho House). Low-pressure booking CTAs | Stock hospitality modifiers ("innovative design", "top-notch", "friendly vibes", "seasonal, locally sourced") |
| **Agency / studio** | Very little self-description. One-line project summaries, about-page conversation, job ads, nav microcopy | Partner/office/headcount numbers, named clients in testimonials, the work itself | Either structural fact with restraint (Pentagram, W+K) or a deliberately funny conversational about page (Ueno, Mother) | The category-noun stack (Instrument's H1) and transform/drive/improve triads (COLLINS) |
| **Personal / portfolio** | The one-line bio, the interests list, essay titles | Names of clients, books, talks, dates. Availability date | Three roles with one deflating adjective (Chimero, Hische). Pure facts with zero adjectives when the facts are strong (Paul Graham) | (No real failures in this sample.) The risk is a credential list with no person in it |
| **Fitness / wellness** | Hero mantra, room/class description, schedule | Calories, %, class length, addresses | The room as a sensory event (Barry's lights, Y7's candles and no mirrors). A twisted aphorism (Equinox "I THINK, THEREFORE I TRAIN") | Self-help clichés ("best version of yourself") and empty superlatives ("Unrivaled") |

Other contrasts:
- **Person and tense.** Cafés and DTC brands say "we" and "you". Hotels describe the place in third person and then switch to "you" for actions. Portfolios use "I", except Paul Graham, who uses third person. Agencies split: Ueno, Mother and COLLINS say "we", Pentagram is institutional "we", and W+K is mostly third person.
- **Humour budget.** Highest for CPG (innocent, Oatly, Tony's) and small agencies (Ueno, Mother). Lowest for luxury (Aman, Aesop, Soho House), where wit becomes understatement ("In appreciation of a father's fiction").
- **Testimonials.** Food and hospitality use critics and press (Dishoom, Y7). SaaS and agencies use named customers with company names (Basecamp, Ueno). DTC uses press snippets as social proof (Allbirds). Luxury uses none on the homepage (Aman, Aesop, Soho House).

### C. Hero-headline patterns the pros actually use

Observed lengths: almost all are 2–9 words, and almost none have more than one clause. None stacks two adjectives.

1. **The greeting / self-introduction.** "Hello, we're innocent" · "Hi! We're Glossier." · "Ueno, you know" · "We are COLLINS." · "We've been expecting you." (Basecamp, aimed at the reader)
2. **The measurable mission.** "together we'll make chocolate 100% slave free" (Tony's)
3. **Product news in plain words.** "Two new ways to blush." (Glossier) · "We're Branching Out" (Allbirds) · "summer better than others" (Sweetgreen)
4. **The question to the reader.** "Where Will Your Cold Brew Bundle Take You?" (Blue Bottle) · "Need a nudge?" (Hoxton) · "Got a project?" (Ueno) · "What?! Slavery?" (Tony's)
5. **The imperative / mantra.** "Do One Thing Well." · "Be narrow." (Hiut) · "Send Better Email" (MailChimp) · "WE FLOW HARD®" (Y7)
6. **The reversal or sequence.** "IT'S NOT FITNESS. IT'S LIFE." (Equinox) · "_skin first. makeup second." (Glossier) · "Body Envy Ends Here." (Barry's)
7. **Place + time anchor.** "The original Hoxton, a firm fixture in Shoreditch since 2006." · "From Bombay with love" (Dishoom) · "Open house hotels, inspired by the streets and scenes that surround them." (Hoxton)
8. **Twisted idiom or pun.** "Wear in the Wild" · "A Fitting Gift" (Allbirds) · "I THINK, THEREFORE I TRAIN" (Equinox) · "Tap that app." (Sweetgreen) · "The nail that stands up cannot be hammered down." (W+K Tokyo)
9. **The narrow, provable superlative.** "Pentagram is the world's largest independently-owned design studio." · "The Best Workout in the World." (Barry's, backed by numbers on the next page)
10. **The hand-made image headline.** Oatly sets its headlines as drawn images and comments on them in the body ("We didn't write that headline because…").

### D. Word-level observations

**Nouns and verbs carry the weight; adjectives are sensory or literal.**
- Adjectives that survive are physical or measurable: stiff, red-tinted, candle-lit, sepia, greaseless, dodgy, minuscule, six-pound, "Citrus, woody, herbaceous".
- Evaluative adjectives (amazing, incredible, ingenious) appear mostly in irony. Oatly: "this incredible computerized oatmilk locator", "the ingenious name Oatfinder".
- Proper nouns are everywhere: Kolshitsky, Peugeot 505, Ladbroke Grove, Borobudur, Mount Fuji, Kumano Kodo, Ponce City Market, Babington House.
- Verbs are physical and specific: warm, inhale, stare, smell, shave (45 seconds off), raid (the pantry), parade, lace up, overlook, reside, squint.
- Coined or house words show a human hand: procrastiworking, cappoatccino, chocoshop, bananaphone, Fruit Towers, Head of Mothering, silent sports, No Wash Club.
- Brackets and asides carry the voice: "(We don't recommend this.)", "(really!)", "(We love that part too.)", "(Sorry.)", "(and because we enjoy it)".

**Conspicuously absent words.** Checked by text search across all ~60 fetched pages, nav, footer and legal text included.
- **Zero occurrences anywhere:** leverage, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art, bespoke, handcrafted, best-in-class, high-quality.
- **Present only in the weaker spots or in quotes from others:**
  - "innovative": Freehand, MailChimp's corporate line, and innocent, where it's undercut by the joke that follows: kids' fruit tubes are "An innovative way to sneak fruit into people"
  - "empower": MailChimp corporate line only
  - "elevate(d)": only in a client testimonial on Ueno, and literally as "heart-rate elevated" on Barry's
  - "seamless": only in Glossier product/legal text
  - "curated": once, about an Ace minibar
  - "world-class", "unrivaled", "unparalleled": Equinox only
  - "revolutionary": Allbirds, Glossier press quote, Warby Parker. Each time it's the weakest sentence on the page.
  - "luxury": in Aman's visible body copy, zero times. Only in the SEO title and meta.
- **Words that read as LLM tells today turn up only literally in this pre-LLM set:**
  - "tapestry" appears once: innocent describing an actual tapestry a woman sent them ("a bit like the Turin Shroud, but less old")
  - "delve" appears once: MailChimp inviting you to "Delve deep into a mystical world of boozy delight" (about cocktails)
  - "journey" appears mostly as literal travel (Stumptown's four-day trips, Aman itineraries) or a product feature (Tony's "bean to bar journey")
  - "unique" is used once by Pentagram and then immediately proved
- **Other abstractions the strong pages avoid:** solutions (Patagonia uses it literally: "solutions to the environmental crisis"), synergy, ecosystem (only Glossier and Barry's, where it reads as the weakest line), "experience" as a noun in headlines (it appears in meta/SEO text and agency boilerplate, rarely in a hero), passionate/passion (mostly in hiring or about boilerplate, never in a hero), "elevate your".

### E. How the about / story sections are written

1. **They start in a specific moment, not with a thesis.** Blue Bottle opens in 1683 Vienna. innocent opens at a music festival stall with two bins. Warby Parker opens with glasses lost on a backpacking trip. Mother opens around a lunch table. Allbirds opens with "A native of New Zealand, Tim Brown". Stumptown opens with a mug warming your hand.
2. **They're structured as anecdotes or questions, not value pillars.**
   - innocent's About is a joke-headed timeline ("The fairly boring bit", "How to go bankrupt, quickly").
   - Blue Bottle uses questions and numbers as headings ("319 years later", "May we bring you coffee?", "Who are our people?").
   - Hiut uses mantras ("Be narrow.", "Grand Masters.").
   - The weakest one (Sweetgreen) is the one that uses "Our Core Values / Our Mission" pillars full of abstract nouns.
3. **Failure and luck are part of the plot.** Rejections by every bank (innocent), a "fraught business model" (Blue Bottle), "Thank you Mr Pinto" (innocent's lucky investor), a self-published book (Chimero).
4. **Founders are characters, not titles.** "a slightly disaffected freelance musician and coffee lunatic" (Blue Bottle). "an itinerant climber selling pitons from the back of his car" (Patagonia). Compare COLLINS's founder bio, a list of awards and boards. It's impressive and nobody remembers it.
5. **Values come as behaviours with numbers.** Stumptown pays "up to four times" the commodity price. innocent gives "10% of our annual profits" to its foundation and admits once giving away 46% and nearly going bankrupt. Patagonia gives "at least 1% of our sales". Soho House requires two nominations.
6. **They end on a modest present tense, not a grand vision.** "Improbably and delightfully, there are hundreds of us now." (Blue Bottle). "We're just getting started." (COLLINS). "Hope you are okay with that." (Oatly). "That's it really." (Ueno). "Come on in." (Mother).
7. **They name the team and how to reach them.** Blue Bottle names its four support staff. Oatly names Chelsea. Ueno and W+K list street addresses. innocent lists Fruit Towers and the "bananaphone".
8. **Portfolio about pages follow a recognisable shape:**
   - one-line bio with three roles and a deflating adjective
   - one paragraph of what they make and for whom
   - one or two signature projects with dates
   - a credo sentence
   - a list of odd interests
   - availability and contact
   - Chimero's page follows this exactly. Paul Graham's is the facts-only version.
