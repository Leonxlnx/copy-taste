[H2] The espresso machine left on a flatbed in March 2019.
Vesna Kovač opened in November 2018 with a leased two-group espresso machine and a menu board listing twenty-two drinks. She had spent eleven years testing drinking water for the City of Hamilton and no time at all making lattes, and the lattes showed it. By February she was three payments behind.
The leasing company's flatbed came on a Tuesday at seven in the morning, in the middle of the busiest hour she'd had. She asked the driver to wait while she finished the order in front of her. He unplugged the machine anyway.
That afternoon she borrowed the batch brewer from the Croatian hall on Barton Street, where it had made coffee for christenings and funerals for about forty years, and bought two glass pots at a restaurant supply down the road. She wrote the brew time on each pot in grease pencil so she would know when to pour it out.
A few people asked where the latte had gone. Most of them came back the next morning, and plenty of them still do. She returned the hall's brewer in 2020, bought two of her own, and took the twenty-two drinks off the wall. The board has had three lines on it ever since.

[H2] 2,113 tests passed. The Android app still broke.
In February 2024 I was a backend engineer at a ferry-ticketing company on the west coast of Norway. I changed one field in our fares API, price , from an integer in øre to a decimal string in kroner. The change was correct. All 2,113 tests agreed with me, and code review took four minutes.
Our Android app parsed that field as an integer. For 31 hours it showed every crossing as costing NaN kr , and the ticket office in Stavanger taped a handwritten price list to its window until we shipped a fix.
No test caught it because no test sent the request the app sent. Production sent it about 40,000 times a day. The first Echolot was a script on my laptop that replayed one hour of those requests from a packet capture against my branch and printed what came back different. The script is long gone. The rule it enforced is the whole product: before you merge, find out what your change does to the requests people send today.
Gunnhild Aas Founder, Bergen, November 2026
==== ====
[H2] Add the recorder, then open pull requests as usual
Echolot needs two things from you: a place to copy traffic from, and a build of your branch it can send requests to. If you already run preview environments, you have the second one.
[H3] Record
Run echolot-recorder as a sidecar, or as an Envoy tap next to your service. It copies each request and response after the response is written, so it never sits in the request path. The default sample is 1 request in 100.
[H3] Scrub
Scrubbing runs inside your cluster, before anything is uploaded. Cookies and Authorization headers are dropped. Fields listed under redact become typed placeholders, so an email address still looks like an email address to your validation code.
[H3] Replay
When a pull request opens, Echolot sends the last 24 hours of samples to three targets: your PR build and two separate instances of main.
[H3] Compare
Echolot diffs every response, removes the fields that differed between the two main instances, and posts what is left as one comment on the pull request. Push again and the comment updates in place.

[H2] Echolot works out which fields are supposed to change
Send the same request twice and you rarely get the same bytes back. Timestamps move, request IDs are random, and lists come back in whatever order the query planner chose that millisecond.
Most response-diffing tools ask you to write ignore rules for all of it, and the rules go stale by the next sprint. Echolot sends every recorded request to main twice. Any field that differs between those two responses is noise for that endpoint and drops out of the comparison.

Arrays are compared as sets when the two main instances return the same items in a different order. If order is part of your contract, set ordered: true on that endpoint.
Summary mode groups changes by endpoint and field, so 1,904 identical timezone diffs become one line with a count. Full mode lists every request, which is useful for about a day while you tune redact .

The two-copies-of-main trick comes from Diffy, which Twitter open-sourced in 2015. Diffy left recording traffic and reporting results to you. Echolot does both, and runs on every pull request.

[H2] What Echolot won't replay
Some traffic can't be sent twice safely, and some can't be compared in any useful way. This is the current list, and it changes with each release.
POST, PUT, PATCH and DELETE, by default
A replayed write is a second booking. Once your PR build points at a sandbox database, you can turn writes on per route with methods and write_guard: sandbox .
WebSockets and server-sent events
Echolot compares complete responses, and a stream has no end to compare against. Support is planned, with no date yet.
Requests that mean something different tomorrow
A query for "departures in the next hour" recorded yesterday asks about yesterday. If your service accepts a fixed clock in tests, pass it through the X-Echolot-Recorded-At header. If it doesn't, Echolot marks those endpoints as unreliable and leaves them out of the comment.
Services with very little traffic
Below about 2,000 requests a day, a 1% sample misses rare endpoints. Raise sample , or check in a fixture file of requests and replay that.
GitLab and Bitbucket
GitHub only for now. GitLab support is in beta with four teams. Bitbucket is not on the roadmap.

[H3] Questions teams ask before they install
Does production data leave our network?
On Free and Team, scrubbed samples go to our replay workers in AWS eu-north-1 (Stockholm) and are deleted after 7 days. Scrubbing happens in your cluster first: cookies and Authorization headers stay behind, and fields under redact leave as placeholders. On Self-hosted, nothing leaves.
How do replayed requests authenticate?
Captured credentials are dropped at record time, so Echolot replays with a service token you give it. If your API returns different data per tenant, map a test user to each tenant with auth.per_tenant .
Which languages and frameworks does it support?
Echolot works at the HTTP and gRPC layer, so your language doesn't come into it. Current customers run it in front of Go, Kotlin, Rails, Django, Node and Elixir services.
We don't have preview environments. Can we still use it?
Yes. Echolot can build one from your Dockerfile, with a docker-compose.yml for databases and queues. Expect about six extra minutes per pull request. This won't work if your service needs managed cloud resources to boot.
Will the recorder slow down production?
The recorder copies traffic after each response is written, on its own thread, into a bounded buffer. When the buffer fills, it drops samples. It never blocks a request.
How do we cancel?
Team is billed monthly. Cancel on the billing page and the recorder stops uploading at the end of the period. Your echolot.yml stays in your repo in case you come back.

[H2] The night the last train to Brig didn't run
Rosalba Ferraris ran Bar Sempione, on the corner of the station square, for twenty-two years before there was a hotel above it. One Thursday in February 1996 the last train to Brig was cancelled a few minutes before it was due. The waiting room was locked at midnight, and the eleven people left on platform 1 crossed the square to the only lit window, which was hers.
She took them upstairs to her flat. Four slept in the two beds, three on the sofa and the floor, three at the kitchen table with their heads on their arms, and one, a cheese buyer from Bern called Herr Zbinden, in the bath, with a pillow (he asked for the bath). In the morning she made eleven coffees on the bar machine and walked them across to the 6:12. Herr Zbinden left 50,000 lire under his saucer with a note: Für das Zimmer. For the room.
She opened four rooms the next spring. That first year, room 5 sat directly above the freight sidings, and nobody who was given it got any sleep. It holds luggage now. The bar closed in 2011 after losing money for longer than the family likes to admit; breakfast is served where the counter used to be.
Rosalba is 84. Her son Ettore runs the desk and writes most of the slips. She still does breakfast on Saturdays, which is market day in Domodossola, and the torta di pane is hers.
In Italian station announcements, a coincidenza is a connecting train. Rosalba liked the other meaning as well.
